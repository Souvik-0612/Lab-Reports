Nuclear medicine departments handle unsealed radionuclides for diagnostic imaging and targeted therapy. Unlike sealed-source applications, the radiopharmaceutical can spread outside the vial through preparation errors, spills, aerosols, patient excreta, contaminated disposables, and poor workflow segregation. Therefore, \textit{radiation survey} and \textit{contamination monitoring} are not supplementary tasks but foundational radiation protection operations that support safe daily clinical practice \cite{cherry2012physics,iaea_ssg46,aerb_rpr2004}.

\subsection{Nuclear Medicine Department Design and Layout}

The architectural design and layout of a nuclear medicine department are fundamental in executing effective radiation protection protocols, specifically the principles of time, distance, and shielding. A well-planned layout minimizes the crossing paths of radioactive sources, injected patients, and non-radiation workers or the general public \cite{iaea_ssg46}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Figures/Experimental/NM department layout.jpg}
    \caption{Typical layout and room design of a Nuclear Medicine Department showcasing the segregation of active and non-active zones.}
    \label{fig:nm_layout}
\end{figure}

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:nm_layout}, a typical department is broadly categorized into:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unrestricted/Non-Active Areas:} Reception, waiting rooms for non-injected patients, staff offices, and reporting rooms. These areas require no special radiation monitoring and are generally accessible.
    \item \textbf{Supervised Areas:} Rooms where occupational exposure conditions need to be kept under review, but special procedures are not normally needed. This includes corridors connecting active rooms or camera control rooms.
    \item \textbf{Controlled/Active Areas:} Areas where specific protective measures and safety provisions are required for controlling normal exposures or preventing the spread of contamination. These include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textit{Radiopharmacy / Hot Lab:} The most critical area where unsealed sources are stored, eluted, and dispensed. It typically features L-bench shields, fume hoods, and strict access control.
        \item \textit{Injection / Dose Administration Room:} Where the prepared dose is administered to the patient.
        \item \textit{Post-Injection Waiting Area:} A dedicated room or shielded cubicles for patients to wait while the radiopharmaceutical distributes in their body. These patients are active radiation sources and must be isolated from non-injected patients.
        \item \textit{Imaging Rooms (PET/SPECT):} Housing the scanner gantries.
        \item \textit{Active Toilets:} Specially plumbed designated toilets for injected patients to safely dispose of radioactive bodily fluids, often leading to delay-and-decay tanks.
        \item \textit{Radioactive Waste Storage:} Secure areas for the decay of contaminated syringes, vials, and consumables prior to safe disposal \cite{aerb_publications}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The workflow is designed to be unidirectional wherever possible—from receiving the isotope, to the hot lab, to patient injection, to the scan room, and finally to patient exit—preventing the backward tracking of contamination into clean zones.

\subsection{Protection Philosophy and Regulatory Basis}
The theoretical basis of this experiment is rooted in three internationally accepted principles:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Justification:} a medical radiation procedure should provide net benefit;
    \item \textbf{Optimization (ALARA):} exposure is kept as low as reasonably achievable considering social and economic factors;
    \item \textbf{Dose limitation for workers/public:} systematic controls are required for non-patient exposures.
\end{itemize}
These principles are articulated by ICRP and implemented in IAEA safety requirements for medical uses of ionizing radiation \cite{icrp103,iaea_gsr3,iaea_ssg46}. In India, institutional implementation is aligned with AERB's regulatory framework and the Atomic Energy (Radiation Protection) Rules \cite{aerb_rpr2004,aerb_publications,aerb_rfsc_2025}.

For a nuclear medicine unit, this translates into a practical question: \textit{Is radioactivity staying where it is intended to be?} Area survey answers the external field component, while contamination survey answers the source control component.

\subsection{Concept of Area Survey}
Area survey is the measurement of ambient radiation field in and around working zones. Typical monitored points include dose preparation benches, dispensing counters, syringe transport routes, uptake rooms, imaging gantry surroundings, patient toilets, waste decay storage, and nearby unrestricted corridors.

From a physics perspective, measured quantity is usually ambient dose equivalent rate (or an operationally equivalent meter reading), reported in $\mu$Sv/h. Repeated measurements over time establish a baseline map. Deviations from baseline are often more informative than a single reading because they indicate process drift, unexpected source accumulation, or shielding compromise \cite{iaea_ssg46,bushberg2011essential}.

Area survey data are used to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item classify and verify controlled/supervised regions,
    \item optimize occupancy and staff traffic routes,
    \item validate shielding assumptions in routine operation, and
    \item detect unusual source retention (for example in waste holding or uptake areas).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Concept of Contamination Survey}
Contamination monitoring addresses the spatial transfer of unsealed activity from intended containment surfaces to unintended surfaces. In practice, contamination is classified as:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Fixed contamination:} activity strongly bound to the surface, difficult to remove by gentle wiping;
    \item \textbf{Removable contamination:} activity transferred to gloves/swipes and therefore immediately relevant for spread and internal exposure pathways.
\end{itemize}

The key radiological concern of removable contamination is pathway formation. Once transferred to skin, clothing, handles, phones, records, or food-contact surfaces, exposure risk shifts from external-only to combined external plus potential internal intake (ingestion/inhalation). Hence removable contamination has high operational significance even when total activity appears small \cite{cherry2012physics,iaea_ssg46}.

\subsection{Detector Physics Relevant to the Experiment}
\subsubsection{GM-Based Contamination Monitoring}
Geiger-Muller detectors are widely used for contamination checks because of high sensitivity and fast response. In Geiger operation, one initiating ionization event triggers a full avalanche; pulse amplitude is nearly independent of deposited energy. Therefore, count rate is suitable for detection and screening but not for precise dosimetry. Important limitations include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item dead-time losses at high count rates,
    \item energy dependence,
    \item geometry dependence for extended/non-uniform contamination,
    \item limited radionuclide discrimination.
\end{itemize}
These effects must be considered when interpreting high-count situations near concentrated activity \cite{knoll2010radiation}.

\subsubsection{Ionization-Chamber Area Survey}
Ionization chamber meters operate in the saturation region with no gas multiplication, so signal is proportional to ion pairs created in the chamber volume. This gives improved dose-rate fidelity and better behavior in moderate-to-high field conditions compared with GM counters. Hence ion chambers are preferred for quantitative area radiation survey, while GM meters remain practical for contamination tracing \cite{podgorsak2005radiation,bushberg2011essential}.

\subsection{Mathematical Basis for Wipe-Test Quantification}
For removable contamination, a known area $S$ is wiped and counted. If measured count rate is $C_{\text{gross}}$ and background is $C_{\text{bg}}$ (in CPM), then
\begin{equation}
    C_{\text{net}} = C_{\text{gross}} - C_{\text{bg}}.
\end{equation}

Estimated removable activity on the wipe is:
\begin{equation}
    A_{\text{rem}} = \frac{C_{\text{net}}}{60\,\epsilon\,f},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon$ is counting efficiency and $f$ is wipe recovery fraction.

Area-normalized removable contamination is:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{rem}} = \frac{A_{\text{rem}}}{S} \quad (\text{Bq/cm}^2).
\end{equation}

If counting is delayed by $\Delta t$ after sampling for radionuclide decay constant $\lambda$, the decay-corrected activity at sampling time is:
\begin{equation}
    A_0 = A_{\text{rem}}e^{\lambda \Delta t}.
\end{equation}

These relationships show that contamination result quality depends not only on detector response but also on wipe technique, counting geometry, radionuclide calibration, and timing control \cite{cherry2012physics,knoll2010radiation,iaea_ssg46}.

\subsection{Minimum Detectable Activity and Practical Sensitivity}
In low-level contamination work, non-detection does not imply zero activity. The practical statement is that activity is below the system's detection capability under given counting time and background. A longer counting time, optimized detector-window selection, and stable background can significantly lower the detectable threshold. Therefore, routine programs should keep measurement conditions consistent so trend comparison is meaningful across survey days \cite{knoll2010radiation,iaea_ssg46}.

\subsection{Survey Design in Nuclear Medicine Workflow}
A scientifically sound survey program is risk-based rather than random. Sampling density is highest at transfer points where activity changes hands or containers. Typical priority nodes are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item dose drawing and assay bench,
    \item syringe shielding and transport trays,
    \item injection chairs and patient hand-contact surfaces,
    \item floor zones near waste bins and sink drains,
    \item patient toilets and release pathways,
    \item decay storage interfaces.
\end{itemize}

The same route should be repeated with a fixed sequence, probe distance, and counting dwell to reduce operator-dependent variability. Mapping data on a floor plan improves interpretability and supports corrective action prioritization \cite{iaea_ssg46,aerb_rfsc_2025}.

\subsection{Response Logic for Contamination Events}
When elevated contamination is found, response follows a control hierarchy:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item isolate and label the affected area,
    \item prevent spread through controlled movement and PPE,
    \item perform staged decontamination,
    \item re-survey after each stage,
    \item record final status and probable root cause.
\end{enumerate}

Repeated re-survey is essential because a single post-cleaning reading can be misleading due to residual wetness, detector positioning, or temporary redistribution. Event documentation should include location, radionuclide (if known), first reading, decontamination method, final reading, and preventive recommendation \cite{iaea_ssg46,aerb_rpr2004}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Sources and Measurement Limitations}
Theoretical interpretation of survey data requires explicit recognition of uncertainties:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Statistical fluctuation:} counting follows Poisson behavior, dominant at low count levels;
    \item \textbf{Instrument factors:} calibration status, dead time, battery condition, energy dependence;
    \item \textbf{Geometric factors:} probe-to-surface distance, orientation, and source non-uniformity;
    \item \textbf{Sampling factors:} wipe pressure, area definition, surface roughness, moisture, recovery factor;
    \item \textbf{Temporal factors:} decay during delay before counting.
\end{itemize}

Hence, reporting should avoid false precision. A good practice is to combine numeric results with categorical interpretation (background level, elevated but controlled, contamination event requiring intervention) aligned with institutional action criteria \cite{knoll2010radiation,iaea_ssg46}.

\subsection{Quality Assurance and Record-Based Safety}
Radiation protection in nuclear medicine is fundamentally a record-driven control system. Trend plots of area dose rate and contamination results provide early-warning information before a major event occurs. QA elements include instrument constancy checks, periodic calibration traceability, defined survey frequency, documented action levels, and training refreshers for all handlers of unsealed sources \cite{aerb_publications,iaea_gsr3,iaea_ssg46}.

In this context, the experiment demonstrates not only measurement technique but also the operational philosophy of nuclear medicine safety: detect early, intervene promptly, verify objectively, and document completely.

\subsection{Summary of Theoretical Significance}
This experiment integrates detector physics, contamination transfer mechanisms, and regulatory safety philosophy into one practical framework. Area survey quantifies external radiation fields; contamination survey verifies source confinement; swipe quantification links count data to activity metrics; and regulatory guidance ensures that measurements are translated into defensible safety actions. Together, these components form the scientific and operational basis for radiation protection in nuclear medicine departments \cite{icrp103,iaea_gsr3,iaea_ssg46,aerb_rpr2004,aerb_rfsc_2025}.
