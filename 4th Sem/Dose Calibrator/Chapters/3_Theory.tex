The dose calibrator is one of the most essential instruments in nuclear medicine for measuring and verifying the radioactivity of radiopharmaceuticals before administration to patients. A typical radioisotope calibrator contains an ionization chamber, a high-voltage power supply, an electronic amplifier, and a display unit to select the radioisotope to be calibrated.

The ionization chamber used for the dose calibrator is generally cylindrical well type in shape and contains Argon gas under high pressure ($\sim 20$--$30$ atm). The geometric goal of the well-type design is to approximate ideal $4\pi$ geometry. The highly pressurized gas creates favorable conditions for ionization, resulting in high detector efficiency. The calibrator has two coaxial cylindrical electrodes maintained at a voltage difference from a suitable supply.

In the associated electrometer, the ionization current is processed and displayed in units of activity, commonly in digital form. Lead shielding around the ionization chamber protects personnel from radiation hazards and reduces the background contribution in measurement. A sample holder and a removable liner (which can be cleaned in case of contamination) are usually provided.

When a radioactive vial or syringe is placed into the dose calibrator well, the Argon gas gets ionized. Ion pairs migrate toward the anode and cathode, generating a current proportional to radioisotope activity. Since this current is very small (typically in $\mu$A range), a highly sensitive electrometer is required. Output is displayed in mCi or MBq.

\subsection{Tests Performed}

\subsubsection{Accuracy Test}
The accuracy test signifies how closely an instrument reading matches a known value. A standard source with known activity at a reference date and time is used. The decay-corrected activity at measurement time is calculated and compared with measured activity. Measured values should be within $\pm 5\%$ of the standard value.

\subsubsection{Constancy Test}
Constancy is a routine quality control test to ensure reproducibility. A long-lived standard source (for example $^{137}$Cs) is measured repeatedly (about 5--10 times). Deviation between repeated measurements should be minimal, and values should remain within tolerance.

\subsubsection{Linearity Test}
This test checks whether measured activity follows expected decay over a wide range (from high activity down to low activity). A source such as $^{99m}$Tc or $^{18}$F-FDG is measured at fixed time intervals, and measured values are compared with expected decay-corrected values.

\subsubsection{Geometry Test}
This test evaluates whether measured activity changes with sample geometry. A known activity is measured while changing source volume (by serial dilution with saline). Ideally, readings should remain nearly constant if the response is geometry independent for the chosen container and setup.
