3 Theory
Radiochromic film dosimetry is widely used in modern radiotherapy because it provides high spatial
resolution, near water-equivalent response, broad dynamic range, and self-developing behavior without
wet chemical processing [1, 2]. For small fields, steep dose gradients, MLC-modulated deliveries, and
geometric QA tests, film remains one of the most informative 2D dosimeters. The practical workflow
used in this experiment follows recommended procedures from Radiochromic.com tutorials, peer-reviewed
protocol papers, and the EBT3 product guidance published by Ashland [3, 4, 5, 6].

3.1 Fundamental Physical Principle
GAFchromicEBT3 film consists of an active microcrystalline layer between polyester substrates.
Ionizing radiation initiates polymerization of sensitive monomers in the active layer, creating color cen-
ters that increase optical absorbance. The color change is dose-dependent and can be digitized by a
flatbed scanner. Since EBT3 is nearly tissue equivalent, perturbation of the radiation field is small for
megavoltage dosimetry [1].
For EBT3 specifically, the manufacturer describes the film as suitable for fast and highly accurate
radiotherapy measurements with high-resolution images, while protocol studies recommend standardized
reflection scanning and consistent film handling to reduce readout error [6, 5].

3.2 Scanner-Based Readout and Image Formation
Film response is not measured directly in Gy; instead, scanner pixel values in red, green, and blue
channels are acquired (typically 48-bit RGB, 16-bit/channel). Accurate dosimetry therefore depends on
a stable dosimetric chain:
film lot,
irradiation geometry,
scanner model and settings,
analysis algorithm.
Important scanner effects include orientation dependence and the lateral response artifact (LRA).
LRA appears as position-dependent scanner response along the lamp axis and can bias dose if calibration
regions are not centered or corrected [7]. Hence, protocol-driven scanning is essential: warm-up scans,
fixed orientation, centered placement, repeated scans (first discarded), and identical acquisition settings
for calibration and measurement [3, 4, 5].
3.3 Calibration Theory and Dose Conversion
3.3.1 Need for Lot-Specific Calibration
Radiochromic film sensitivity varies by manufacturing lot and changes slowly with storage conditions
(time, temperature, humidity). Therefore, calibration must be lot-specific and repeated periodically.
Calibration doses must cover the complete clinical dose range to avoid extrapolation error [3, 1].

3.3.3 Practical Calibration Sequence
The recommended sequence is:
1. Cut one sheet into multiple strips while preserving orientation marks.
2. Irradiate strips to known reference doses, including one unexposed strip (0 Gy).
3. Choose dose points from low dose to about 120% of maximum expected clinical dose.
4. Scan strips together at the scan center with fixed geometry.
5. Assign ROIs with homogeneous dose and fit calibration model.
Uncertainty in reference dose (usually from ion chamber traceability) propagates directly into film
dose uncertainty; therefore, careful reference dosimetry is mandatory.
3.4 Determination of Unknown Dose
After calibration, the unknown irradiated film is scanned under the same protocol. Pixel values are
converted to dose point-by-point through the fitted calibration function. If needed, inter-scan correction
and dose-rescaling options are applied with central unexposed/known-dose reference ROIs [4].

3.5 Spoke (Starshot) Test: Geometric QA Principle
The spoke or starshot film test evaluates radiation isocenter size and stability. Multiple narrow beams
are delivered at different gantry/collimator angles to produce a star pattern on film. Ideally, all beam
axes intersect at a common point; deviations indicate mechanical/radiation misalignment.
From analyzed spoke lines, the software estimates:
radiation isocenter coordinates,
isocenter radius (or diameter),
displacement relative to fiducial/laser marker.
A smaller reconstructed isocenter radius indicates better geometric precision of treatment delivery,
which is critical for IMRT/VMAT and stereotactic techniques.
3.6 Field Congruence Test (Light vs Radiation Field)
Field congruence verifies whether the light field projected by the treatment unit coincides with the
actual radiation field delivered to the film. In QA terms, this checks setup fidelity between optical
positioning and dose delivery.
Profiles across field edges are analyzed to locate radiation boundaries (often by 50% dose level/FWHM
for dose maps, or inflection-point approximation for raw images). Differences between light-field marks
and radiation boundaries are reported as directional displacements on each semiaxis. Significant dis-
agreement can cause geometric targeting errors and requires machine adjustment.

3.8 Sources of Uncertainty and Their Control
Major uncertainty components in film dosimetry are:
Film related:lot variability, handling marks, orientation inconsistency, post-irradiation growth.
Irradiation related:setup reproducibility, phantom alignment, reference-dose uncertainty.
Scanning related:warm-up instability, LRA, inter-scan variability, film curling, nonuniform con-
tact.
Analysis related:ROI selection bias, registration error, model fitting uncertainty, inappropriate
extrapolation.
Control measures include strict orientation marking, fixed post-irradiation scanning time (typically
around 24 h), centralized ROIs, repeated scans with first-scan rejection, multichannel calibration, and
consistent correction strategy [1, 3, 4].
3.9 Clinical Relevance
The combined use of calibration-based unknown dose determination, spoke test, field congruence test,
and patient-specific QA provides bothdosimetricandgeometricverification in radiotherapy. Film
dosimetry therefore acts as an end-to-end QA tool linking machine performance, treatment planning
accuracy, and delivery fidelity, ultimately supporting safer and more accurate patient treatments.
